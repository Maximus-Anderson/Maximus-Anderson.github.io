CR30 Powertrain: Troubleshooting Through Competition

May-June 2026 · Powertrain Inverter Lead · ~8 min read

What it took to get Cyclone Racing's CR30 driving reliably after the pack was built, from a blown inverter to our first EV competition.

The Setup

  • Motor: EMRAX 208 HV
  • Inverter: DTI HV-550
  • Competition: passed battery + mechanical tech

Context

This post covers getting CR30's motor and inverter to make reliable power after the pack was built. The motor is an EMRAX 208. The inverter is a DTI HV-550, which turns the battery's DC into the three-phase AC that drives the motor. The pack build is in CR30 Battery Pack: Design and Manufacture and the cooling design is in CR30 Battery Pack: Thermal Optimization for Endurance.

The Blown Inverter

Near the end of CR30's second driving day the motor made a loud whine and the inverter stopped working. An inverter drives the motor through six power switches called IGBTs. They come in three modules of two, one module per motor phase. One switch had failed, so that module had to be replaced.

The motor had never been tuned. I had handed the motor setup to our motor engineer and didn't check it before we drove. I never confirmed what killed the IGBT, but running power through an untuned motor is the most likely cause.

A replacement inverter was quoted at £1,400 with a four week lead time. Competition started June 16, so waiting four weeks would have left almost no time to test. I opened the inverter and looked for the failure with a multimeter. In diode mode the meter reads the voltage drop across a switch, and I measured from the DC input to each phase terminal. Five switches read 0.32 V and the sixth read about 0.2 V. Testing the gate of that sixth IGBT showed that its gate driver, the part that switches it on and off, had failed too.

Digikey sells both the IGBT module and the gate driver. I bought one of each and moved the gate resistors from the old driver to the new one so the new module would switch the same way as the other two. The repair cost about $300, and the inverter ran again.

The Power Cutout

With the inverter repaired, the car had a new problem. Partway through a hard acceleration it cut power, and it wouldn't pull again until it came to a full stop. The high voltage stayed on and the inverter logged no fault.

The BMS was also reporting weak cell and open cell faults at the time. Those came from the pogo pins on the battery management boards, or BMBs. A pogo pin is a spring-loaded contact, and each one reads the voltage of one cell. The pins were compressed further than the design accounted for, so they stuck and lost contact with the cells. I replaced the pins with jumper wires that run from the board to fork terminals under the bolts already on the cells.

I rebuilt one board first and ran it on a test day. That module had no faults, so I rebuilt the other four.

The BMS faults were gone but the cutout wasn't. I checked the current limits, the power limit, the switching frequency, the BMS, and low voltage supply sag one at a time, and none of them caused the cutout.

Inverter Telemetry

The car had no way to record what the inverter was doing, so I added a data logger in the middle of a test day. The logger is an Arduino Uno with a SparkFun CAN shield and a microSD card. It reads messages from CAN, the two-wire network the car's electronics use to talk to each other, and writes them to the card. The car had no mount for a logger, so I taped it to the side. On the first runs every inverter value in the excel was a zero.

I had written the logger's firmware in a hurry, so I checked it first and found two problems. The firmware opened and closed the file on the SD card every loop, which dropped samples. It also read only one CAN message per loop, so messages backed up and were lost. I fixed both, and the inverter values were still zero.

Next I wrote a short Arduino program that printed the ID of every message on the CAN bus. The only IDs that printed came from the IMU, the car's motion sensor. The inverter should have been sending eight messages, and none of them showed up.

The inverter has two CAN buses, and it sends its detailed data on the second one. At the inverter's connector, the high wire for the second CAN bus had never been connected. Once that wire was connected, all eight messages appeared and the excel filled with inverter data. That included the phase and DC currents, the temperatures, the faults, and which limit was active.

One problem was left. The CAN bus dropped out during hard acceleration, which is when the inverter switches the most current and makes the most electrical noise. The two CAN wires are twisted together so that noise reaches both wires equally and cancels out. Our pair had about one twist per inch. The shields on the high voltage cables had also been assembled incorrectly and weren't connected to ground. I retwisted the CAN pair to three twists per inch, grounded the shields, and added ferrite cores to the CAN wiring. After that the dropouts were rare, and the inverter data logged to a laptop in the car, which it had never done before.

Flux Linkage and Tuning

With inverter data in the logs I could see what happened during a cutout. The inverter was hitting its input voltage limit, even though the battery voltage was far above that limit.

One setting in the inverter was wrong. A spinning motor generates its own voltage, called back-EMF, and flux linkage is the number that tells the inverter how much back-EMF to expect at a given speed. I measured it by spinning the motor with a Milwaukee drill and reading the back-EMF against RPM. The measured value was about half the value loaded in the inverter, so I corrected it.

The current loop tuning had also been done wrong. Our motor engineer had run the inverter's open loop tuning with the rotor free to spin, and the procedure requires the rotor to be locked. I reran it with the rotor locked, and it showed that the motor's two control axes needed different gains. I also grounded all the cable shields to a single point.

After those changes the car pulled over 50 kW for the first time. I don't know which change mattered, if any.

On the Dyno

On the wheel dyno the car made 56 kW and about 135 Nm at the wheels. The run in the video had no cutout and reached the power limit I had set. Later driving logs peaked in the low 60s of kW. The rules allow 80 kW, and the car unfortunately never officially met that.

Race Prep

The last week before competition went to reliability.

The car lost power every time it turned left. Each high voltage connector carries an interlock, a small circuit that shuts the car down if the connector comes apart. The interlock on the HV+ connector leaving the battery was loose, and cornering load opened it. I replaced that connector and its cable with a new Amphenol one, and the car stopped cutting out in left turns.

The DC-DC converter, which powers the low voltage system from the main battery, was still not working. The car's low voltage battery holds 6 Ah. I built a 13 Ah backup battery and wired it in parallel with the first, which gives 19 Ah and is enough for an endurance run.

The rest of the week went to balancing the pack, which means bringing every cell to the same voltage.

Competition

Competition was our first as an electric team, and nearly all of it went to tech inspection. A car needs six inspection stickers before it can run an event: mechanical, battery and electrical, EV active, tilt, rain, and brakes. The last three are easy to pass once a car is through EV active. We got two stickers and were stopped at EV active.

We passed mechanical tech without much trouble. Most of our time went to the battery and electrical station, on two rules we hadn't fully understood. The high voltage system, called the tractive system or TS, has to be kept separate from the low voltage system. High voltage parts also need two layers of insulation. We added insulation at the event to meet the second rule.

The precharge board failed the first rule. Precharge charges the inverter's capacitors slowly through a resistor before the main contactors close, so there is no surge of current. The board the electrical team built connected the battery side to the tractive system side through an isolated gate, and those two are supposed to be fully separate. On the insulation resistance test that path read about 5 MΩ where it should have read open. Our software engineer Aaron and I spent the night reworking the same board to run precharge over CAN with no analog signal. That was only possible overnight because the CAN work on the car was already done.

The isolation checks still took several tries to pass. My best guess is that the carbon filled baskets leaked a small current from module to module, but I never confirmed it, because it mysteriously went away after the competition and I couldn't replicate it.

The last thing between us and passing the battery portion of tech was charging, and technically we didn't complete it. While demonstrating charging, the discharge resistor overheated and melted its enclosure. The discharge circuit is intended to only bleed the inverter's capacitors when the shutdown circuit opens, which effectively means the pack is off. Its relay is supposed to open whenever shutdown is high and the pack is live. The relay wasn't opening, so the resistor was attempting to discharge the full pack rather than just the capacitors. That issue seemed to be in part due to an interlock inside the charger which had failed. The charger was an off-the-shelf unit we hadn't designed and couldn't reasonably repair at the competition, so the officials sealed it, which prevented us from using it during the competition, and then waived that section. Regardless, by then there wasn't enough time to complete EV active tech, so we left having passed battery and mechanical tech without running a dynamic event.

Competition

An IGBT in the inverter failed near the end of CR30's second driving day, most likely from running a motor that hadn't been tuned. I found it with a diode test and replaced the IGBT and gate driver for about $300 instead of waiting four weeks for a £1,400 inverter.

The car cut power partway through hard accelerations with the high voltage still on and no fault logged. The limits, the BMS, and the low voltage supply all checked out, and there was no inverter data to show the cause.

I added a CAN data logger to the car in the middle of a test day. A wire for the inverter's second CAN bus had never been connected, so no inverter data reached the logger. Connecting the wire brought the data through. Twisting the CAN pair and grounding the cable shields then stopped the dropouts.

The flux linkage in the inverter was set to about twice the motor's measured value. I corrected it, grounded the shields to one point, and retuned the current loop. The car then pulled over 50 kW for the first time. I don't know which change mattered, if any.

On the wheel dyno the car made 56 kW and about 135 Nm at the wheels.

Passed battery and mechanical tech. An illegal precharge board meant reworking precharge over CAN overnight, and the discharge resistor melted its enclosure during the charging check. We ran out of time before the dynamic events.

CR30 Battery Pack: Design and Manufacture

April 2026 · Design Manufacture Lead · ~13 min read

How Cyclone Racing's CR30 battery pack was built, what went wrong, and what changes for CR31.

Specifications

  • Configuration: 28s per module, 5 modules in series
  • Cells: COSMX CA95B0D0HD (carried from CR29)
  • Pack voltage: 600 V fully charged
  • Mass: ~140 lbs

Context

CR30 is Cyclone Racing's first complete electric car and the first to drive under its own power. Prior to this, there was only the CR29 research car that was never finished, and its pack was only built as a proof of concept. This post covers how the CR30 pack was built. The pack is 140 cells in series, which is near 600 V fully charged. The cells are split into five modules of 28, and the modules sit in a welded aluminum housing that bolts into the chassis.

I was powertrain lead and built the pack. That included the modules, the housing, the high voltage wiring, the chassis mounts, and getting the battery management boards working. The design was shared. My design work was the tabs and slots on the housing, the way the tab blocks are held down, and the retention stack that holds the battery management boards flat.

The cells are the ones bought for CR29. CR29 never ran, and we had no budget for new cells. The cooling design has its own post, CR30 Battery Pack: Thermal Optimization for Endurance, and the CR29 pack build is in CR29 Accumulator: Manufacture.

Module Baskets

Each module sits in a basket, a 3D printed shell that holds the 28 cells, insulates them from the aluminum housing, and keeps them from moving. CR29's baskets were polycarbonate. CR30's are PAHT-CF, a carbon fiber filled nylon that was chosen after impact tests against other filaments. Granville designed the basket and filled out its SES, the structural spreadsheet FSAE requires, but struggled to complete it. I took over to finalize the SES and then manufacture the basket.

PAHT-CF warps when it is printed in a part this large, and since our team doesn't have direct access to 3D printers, it took a month to get a basket I could use. The basket was too big for the beds on my Bambu A1 and another powertrain member's X1 Carbon. After digging around, I used a suspension team member's Voron, which was large enough and had an enclosed chamber but didn't have a chamber heater. On the initial prints the corners lifted off the bed and the part came out short in Z. I printed baskets on the Voron with different print patterns, bed temperatures, and speeds, and every one warped too much to use.

I then borrowed a Bambu H2D, which has a heated chamber, and the baskets warped much less. The parts still shrank, so I scaled the model to make up for it. It took three test prints to tune the scaling, and the final model was 1.25% larger in Z.

Even so, the print quality wasn't perfect and every basket came off the printer with burrs. A burr pressed into a pouch cell can puncture it, so I had to sand all five baskets by hand before assembly.

One side of the basket is open. A plate bolts over that side and holds the cells against the opposite wall. The CAD had about 1 mm of clearance between the plate and the cells. The printed parts had none, and I never found out whether the baskets came out small or the way the cells interfaced with one another caused them to be thicker than modeled. Because of this issue, it takes two people to close a basket, and the plate presses firmly on the cells, sort of like an interference fit. I had already tested cell resistance against pressure for the thermal post and pressure had almost no effect, so the tight fit shouldn't change how the cells perform and was acceptable.

Tab Plates and Tab Blocks

The tab plate holds the cells in each module as well as their electrical connections. Each cell's tabs pass through slots in the plate and fold over between two aluminum tab blocks. Bolting the blocks together clamps the tabs. The plate is a PETG print, and it also holds the module's two terminals, which are Amphenol posts. The bulk of the design came from my mentor Gavin, who used a similar concept on the CR29 module design.

The tab blocks are aluminum because the clamp has to be light and stiff, and aluminum also carries heat away from the tabs.

On CR29 every tab block had a bolt at each end. For CR30 Granville and I wanted one bolt per block, which halves the fastener count. Granville first proposed one plastic cover that clamped down over all the blocks. The rules don't allow plastic in a clamped electrical joint, so that wasn't selected. His second idea was a small plastic key/finger over each block, but the keys had stress concentrations and I didn't trust them to last. I suggested adding a small overhang to the plate. One end of each block sits under the overhang and the other end takes a single bolt, cantilevering the block to provide force along the whole length. That removed half the bolts without much effect on cooling.

The bolts thread into aluminum nuts that sit in pockets in the plate. On CR29 the pockets were loose, so a nut could shift as its bolt tightened and get rounded off. For CR30 I made the pockets an interference fit. I printed test pockets at several sizes, and 0.05 mm of interference let a nut press in without damaging the PETG. The nuts we bought for CR30 had rounder corners than CR29's, and tightening the bolts with a tool still deformed the pockets. I got every joint tight but couldn't go tighter without damaging the pockets more. The resistance of those joints was never measured, but it seemed reasonably low in testing.

The initial plan for CR31 is to weld the cell tabs together, ultrasonically or with a laser. That removes the tab blocks, the nuts, and the bolts.

BMBs

Each module has a battery management board, or BMB, that measures the voltage of every cell. The BMB touches each tab block through a pogo pin, which is a spring-loaded contact. Granville designed the board and the pogo pin layout. I ordered bare boards from JLC, assembled seven of them, and tested them on the modules.

The largest problem was that the boards bent. In Granville's design, a BMB is fastened at both ends and unsupported in the middle, and the pogo pin springs push the board away from the tab blocks. The middle of the board bowed, the center pins lost contact, and the BMS lost those cell readings.

The module already had a retention rod that holds the baskets in place, fed through a printed plastic insulator. I redesigned the insulator as a stiffener that presses down across the middle of the board so the center pins make contact.

This fix initially seemed to work, but later the pogo pins began sticking in the compressed position and had to be replaced with wires and fork terminals. That repair is in CR30 Powertrain: Troubleshooting Through Competition.

Four smaller problems also cost time:

  • Harness termination: The harness gave cell 12, cell 13 negative, and cell 13 their own terminations. In a series string the positive of cell 12 and the negative of cell 13 are the same point, so those two wires have to share one termination. The BMS reported ten cells missing until I made version two of the BMBs and rewired the harness.
  • Missing trace: The production boards were missing one trace, so every board had a jumper wire in its place. That was fixed when version two arrived.
  • Thermistor traces: The thermistor traces share a plane, so a wire end touching a cell during installation can burn a trace. That happened twice. After that I covered the pins with Kapton tape while mounting a board and removed the tape once the board was secured. I moved the components from the damaged board onto a spare.
  • Connector: The sense connector doesn't tolerate misalignment. Plugging it in slightly off arcs the pins and blows fuses. That can't be fixed on this board revision.

The boards were meant to make the sense wiring cleaner than CR29's, which was terminated by hand. They worked after these repairs. I later found the boards were illegal, because the low voltage and tractive system wiring terminate into one connector. That didn't stop us from passing that section at comp, but redesigning them is required for CR31.

Module Lifting Tool

The baskets had no feature for lifting a module out of the housing, and I didn't notice until I had to remove one for service. I designed a lifting tool in about twenty minutes. It attaches through the same holes the vertical retention rods use.

The Aluminum Housing

The housing is a welded aluminum box that holds the five modules, the BMS, and the high voltage wiring. The panels are cut on a waterjet and welded together.

On CR29 the panel edges were flat. Every seam had to be clamped, and we had to pray the panels were square before welding. Even then the walls warped. For CR30 I added tabs along the edges of the outer walls and matching slots in the inner walls. Each tab drops into its slot, so the panels hold themselves in position and need no clamps. That worked on every seam that had tabs and slots. Fitting and welding the housing took over three weeks on CR29 and two days on CR30.

I didn't add tabs and slots at the corners. After welding, one corner was bent inward. That corner is where the high voltage components go, and the bend took away enough space that the planned layout no longer fit.

HV Distribution

CR29 brought high voltage out of the pack through one large Amphenol connector in a corner and used Coroflex cable to connect the modules to each other. CR30 started with the same layout. Partway through the design, a senior team member proposed a printed sleeve in that corner, called the Power Prism, to hold the contactors, the main fuse, and copper busbars. The sleeve packs those parts closer together and shortens the high voltage cable runs.

The sleeve only fit because the busbars could be made smaller. We had been sizing conductors from ampacity tables, and those sizes are larger than this pack needs because it only sees peak current for a few seconds at a time. Recalculating from the conductor's resistance and the heat it produces over an endurance run gave much smaller cross sections.

When the housing corner bent inward, the original sleeve no longer fit. I worked with newer team members to redesign it for the smaller space.

The main contactors currently connect to points near the center of the pack. The module terminals beside the corner are electrically the same points. Connecting there on CR31 would replace most of the cable with busbars, free up the center of the pack, make it harder to plug a connector into the wrong place, and save a little weight.

Chassis Mounts

The pack bolts to the chassis at ten mounts, four at the rear and six at the front. Each mount bolts into an insert that was laid into the carbon fiber monocoque before it was cured.

The four rear inserts came out flush with the surface and those mounts bolted on. The six front inserts stuck out above the surface, so the front mounts sat higher than the mounting points on the pack. The pack was already in position and couldn't move. I lowered each front mount until it sat flush with the pack, which left a gap between the mount and the chassis.

The gap was a problem for the SES. The SES requires at least ten mounts for our geometry, and a mount with a gap under it carries more load, so the front mounts failed the strength check. I made an aluminum spacer for each gap. The spacers were machined, welded to the bottom of each mount, and ground flush. The pack, sitting in the chassis, was then used as a jig to drill the bolt holes so they lined up. With the spacers entered in the SES, the mounts passed.

CR31 will bond the inserts in after the chassis is cured. Bonded inserts come out flush and flat, so the mounts sit where they were designed to.

First Drive

The first drive was planned for March 1 and happened on April 26. Most of the delay came from the electrical boards outside the pack, which are the SSI, precharge, shutdown, and HVI boards. They went through about six revisions before they passed bench tests, and some revisions that passed on the bench failed on the car. One couldn't carry the full shutdown loop. Another used relays with too much coil resistance, which dropped the voltage below what the contactors need to close. The wiring harness couldn't be finished until the boards were final, so every revision pushed the schedule back.

The pack had one incident in that time. During work inside the pack an allen key touched exposed pins on a BMB connector. The pins arced and blew eight cell fuses on the board. I removed the board and replaced the fuses.

The night before the drive, with the car on a stand, the wheels spun backward. I worked on it until 1 a.m. thinking the polarity was wrong. At 7 a.m. another team member found a setting in the inverter that reverses motor direction. Unchecking that setting fixed it...

The first time the contactors closed, the Orion BMS reported an open charging relay. The car has no charging relay... I had apparently left a setting enabled that told the BMS to expect one, and turning the setting off cleared the fault.

Next the inverter cut power because the two accelerator pedal sensors disagreed by more than 20%. We tested each sensor alone to find out whether the difference was real or came from the two channels updating at different rates. We didn't find the cause, but we increased the disagreement threshold to 80%.

The inverter also kept faulting for reasons we couldn't trace. The main board from the electrical team wasn't ready, so we had no logged data to read the faults from. We shortened the fault reset time from 5 s to 1 s so the car would recover quickly and keep driving. That was a workaround and not a fix.

With the workaround the car ran laps for most of the day at a 40 kW limit, and the pack had no faults of its own. The day ended when one cell reached the low voltage limit. During module assembly we had dropped a cell and replaced it without checking the replacement's state of charge. That cell fell from 3.46 V to 3.296 V over the day while the rest stayed near 3.56 V.

It started to rain as we packed up. Not every grommet on the car was sealed yet, so we would have had to stop anyway.

Notes for CR31

The changes planned for the next pack:

  1. Weld the cell tabs. That removes the tab blocks, the nuts, and the bolts.
  2. Tabs and slots on every housing seam, including the corners.
  3. Bond the chassis inserts after cure so they sit flush and need no spacers.
  4. Redesign the BMB with a sturdier sense connector, the correct harness termination, the missing trace, and isolated thermistor traces.
  5. Connect high voltage at the corner modules with short busbars.
  6. Consider a composite housing. Aluminum is heavy for this job, and a materials analysis is pending.
  7. Use Melasta pouch cells if the budget allows. They are 11.5 Ah cells instead of 13 Ah, so the pack would be smaller and lighter.
  8. Match the state of charge of any replacement cell before installing it. One mismatched cell ended CR30's first drive early.
  9. Prototype more. About 70% of CR30's parts had small problems that one test print or foam mockup would have caught.

CR30 Battery Pack: Thermal Optimization for Endurance

October 2025 · Research Design Testing Lead · ~4 min read

How DCIR testing on Cyclone Racing's CR30 cells replaced a liquid cooling system with ten fans.

The Problem with the Spec Sheet

The CR30 battery is five modules of lithium-ion pouch cells, and in an FSAE endurance event it sees current bursts up to 200 A. Current through a cell makes heat in proportion to the cell's internal resistance, called DCIR. To work out how much cooling the pack needed, I needed the DCIR of our cells.

The cell spec sheet listed DCIR as either 1.5 mΩ or 5 mΩ, with no graph and no test conditions. With one value the thermal math showed the pack overheating before the end of endurance. With the other it barely needed cooling. I assumed the worst case and started designing a liquid cooling system. It had U-shaped shims touching the cell faces, a cooling plate, thermal pads, quick disconnects, and a pump loop mounted to the pack.

The liquid design had three problems. It added weight and parts, it wouldn't have cooled the cells much, and a fluid loop is one more thing that can fail on a racecar. I was also designing for a problem I hadn't confirmed. So I stopped and tested the cells myself.

Building the Test Rig

Four things can change a cell's DCIR: the pressure on its faces, its temperature, how fast it is discharged (C-rate), and its state of charge (SoC). I built a rig that could test each one separately, for under $100.

  • Load Bank: Four 0.1 Ω heatsink resistors in a 2s2p arrangement so they heat evenly. The bank measured 0.131 Ω by 4-wire Kelvin sensing.
  • C-Rate Tester: A carbon pile rheostat, which gives an adjustable current draw.
  • Pressure Fixture: Machined steel plates with springs, adjustable from 0 to 8 psi.
  • Current Sensing: An SSA-250 and a current clamp borrowed from the PRISM Solar Car team.

Testing Each Variable

Face Pressure: Ruled Out

I tested face pressure first. With the spring fixture I ran pulse tests at 0, 2, 5, and 8 psi on the cell faces, and DCIR barely moved. Pressure also affects how fast cells age, but the car sees few charge cycles over its life, so aging from low pressure isn't a design concern. I dropped pressure as a variable.

C-Rate: Secondary Effect

In the carbon pile tests DCIR went down as current went up, probably because higher current warms the cell. The effect was small, so I left it out of the thermal simulations.

State-of-Charge: Repeatable and Strong

For state of charge I ran 10 second discharge pulses with a 3 minute rest after each. The pulse shows the immediate voltage drop and the first slow response (the 1RC transient), and the rest shows the slower recovery (2RC). As SoC drops there are fewer lithium ions in the electrolyte and the reaction at the electrode surface slows down, and both raise resistance. The tests matched that. DCIR rose at lower SoC, and the result repeated.

Temperature: The One That Mattered

I didn't have access to the university's temperature chamber, so I wrapped a foldable heat pad around isolated cells. I checked cell temperature with a FLIR camera and an IR spot meter. I ran the same pulse test from 25 to 60°C, and temperature changed DCIR more than any other variable.

DCIR Mapping: The Spec Sheet Was Way Off

Temperature and SoC together accounted for most of the change in DCIR. At operating temperature DCIR dropped as low as 0.7 mΩ, less than half of the spec sheet's lower value of 1.5 mΩ. So the cells make less heat than the spec sheet implies.

I built a 3D surface in MATLAB of DCIR against temperature and SoC, then fit a third-order polynomial to it for the team's thermal simulation.

I ran the thermal model with the measured DCIR and a 1.5x safety factor taken from the RC recovery analysis. The pack's peak temperature stayed well below 60°C, the rules maximum, for a full endurance run. The pack didn't need liquid cooling.

Redesign

Since the cells' own thermal mass was enough, I removed the liquid system: the base plate, U-shims, thermal pads, pump, and radiator. I added ten fans in its place. Five run all the time to cool the electronics in the penthouse, the compartment on top of the pack. The other five sit above the modules and are switched on by the BMS as a backup for the cells. Air comes in over the cell tabs and leaves through the penthouse vents whether or not the cell fans are on. The BMS-switched fans cover problems like a connection that degrades and heats up.

Specific Heat: Freezer Calorimetry

No specific heat data was published for these cells, so the team had been using aluminum's value. I checked that with a calorimetry test made from styrofoam, thermocouples, and my freezer. I ran the test on aluminum blocks first to get a calibration multiplier, then on a cell. The cell averaged 0.954 J/g°C across runs. That is close enough to aluminum that the earlier simulations weren't far off.

Takeaways

The spec sheet wasn't usable for thermal design. It gave two DCIR values with no conditions, and the measured value at operating temperature was lower than both. Testing the cells removed the liquid cooling system, saved its weight, and gave the team a thermal model built on measured data. With a temperature chamber I would fill in the edges of the DCIR map and run higher C-rates than the load bank could reach.

I built a DCIR test rig for under $100 with a load bank, a carbon pile rheostat, a pressure fixture, and borrowed current sensors. I also set the test procedure for face pressure, temperature, C-rate, and state of charge.

1976 Honda CB750F Restoration

Summer 2025 · Diagnosis Rebuild Fabrication · ~3 min read

Reviving a 50-year-old SOHC four: carburetors, electrics, timing, and the start of a cafe racer build.

Why This Bike

I saw a cafe racer that Milezero Racer built from a CB750F Clubsport and wanted one. I also wanted to work on a carbureted engine. I had learned how carburetors work in theory but had never had my hands on one, and fixing a bike that didn't run was a way to learn. The bike I got is a 1976 CB750F that hadn't run in years.

Initial Assessment

The bike didn't run when I got it. The tires were dry rotted, so I built a center stand from scrap wood and replaced them first.

Then I checked the four things an engine needs: spark, compression, air, and fuel.

Compression was 130 to 135 psi on all four cylinders. The SOHC forums give 150 to 180 psi as healthy, 120 as the low end, and under 100 as needing work. So the engine was even across cylinders, which is a good sign, but on the low side. The air filter was torn and I replaced it. The carburetors were the main problem. Their float pins were seized from corrosion, so cleaning alone wasn't going to fix them.

Electrical System

The electrical system had three problems. The headlight and tail light drew too much current, turning on the high beams cut power to the whole bike, and the turn signals didn't work. The previous owner had also installed a lithium battery whose BMS couldn't supply what the bike needed, so I replaced it with an AGM battery.

I traced the harness and found loose and corroded bullet connectors all through it. I cleaned or replaced the bad ones and checked continuity afterward. Spark was weak, but the coils tested fine. The coil boots measured well above spec resistance where they meet the plugs, so I replaced the boots and the plugs.

For lighting I built a headlight from a Suzuki GS850 housing with high-output LEDs, and swapped the tail light for an LED one. Before the swap, switching the lights on dropped the voltage by nearly half a volt. After the swap the draw was negligible, the turn signals worked, and the high beams no longer cut power.

Carburetor Swap

The original carburetors were too corroded to rebuild. I found a donor bike and took its Keihin 7A carbs, which have about the same jetting as the originals. I cleaned them, set the float levels, synced all four, and installed them.

The donor carbs got the bike running, but they have been a problem since. They run rich, clog regularly, and lose their tune. I have retuned them six times and fully rebuilt them four times, resetting the float levels and cleaning the jets each time. The plugs have fouled repeatedly. Fixing the carbs is the top priority.

Timing

With the spark fixed and the carbs on, the bike started, but it ran on three cylinders and lugged to a stop after a few minutes. I checked the static timing with the procedure from the SOHC forums. That uses a 12 V test light on the points while the engine is turned by hand, with top dead center confirmed by the timing marks and a finger over the plug hole. Timing was retarded on cylinders 2 and 3. After I adjusted it, all four cylinders fired. I skipped dynamic timing because the bike ran well after the static adjustment, so I still need to check it.

First Miles and What Still Needs Work

The first long ride was 100 miles and the bike finished it. Several problems are left. Fuel economy is poor because the carbs run rich, and the petcock has to be on reserve to flow enough fuel. The clutch is notchy and needs a rebuild. The left front caliper seeps slowly and leaves a film on the rotor before each ride. The seep hasn't soaked the pad yet, and I don't know why.

What's Next

Reliability comes first: the clutch rebuild, the brakes, and the carbs. After that the cafe build starts. I want to make a seat from carbon fiber nylon to cut weight and clean up the tail. The tank will get a clear coat to protect the steel and keep some of the patina. The mirrors vibrate at speed, so I plan to replace them with lower ones that damp better.

Dyno Cart Testing

April–June 2025 · Design Manufacture Validation · ~3 min read

Adapting Cyclone Racing's legacy IC dyno cart to validate an Emrax 208 motor and DTI HV550 inverter before the team committed to going electric.

Why This Existed

Cyclone Racing was finishing its last season with a combustion (IC) car and starting on its first electric one. We had the Emrax 208 motor, but no chassis to put it in and no way to run it under load. The team had done well with IC cars, so before leaving them we needed to show the electric powertrain worked. That meant running the motor with the battery, BMS, and inverter outside of a car, which meant building something to mount it on.

The team had a dyno cart so old that nobody knew when it was built. It has a Porsche sticker and a Ferrari sticker on the side, so we call it the Porsche Ferrari. The cart was set up for a Yamaha R6 engine and couldn't hold the Emrax as it was.

Reconfiguration

The cart had R6 engine mounts, a gas tank, and IC wiring. It had no low voltage (GLV) power for the EV electronics and none of the interlocks that have to stay closed to keep the high voltage system on. If the GLV battery cut out, the high voltage would shut off with it, so the GLV supply had to be dependable.

I designed the changes so the cart could go back to IC. The IC mounts were removed and replaced with bolted ones, so the cart can switch between the IC and EV setups. The GLV wiring moved to a separate cart, so changing setups only means swapping the harness.

Motor Mount

The Emrax 208 is a flat, pancake-shaped motor, and its bolt pattern matched nothing on the cart. The mount is two lengths of square steel tube that span the full width of the cart and are bolted at both ends. They take the motor's torque. A third piece at 90 degrees picks up three of the four mounting holes on the motor bracket. The mount is simple and stiff.

The Water Brake

A water brake puts a load on the motor. The motor spins a rotor inside a housing full of water, like a pump, and the water's resistance pushes back with torque. The CR27 team had used this water brake for a full power test of the R6, so I assumed it would work for the electric motor.

I should have done hand calculations first. The data sheets had been lost, along with most of what the team knew about the brake, and I'm not confident it can take the load the Emrax produces. It was enough for these tests. Future tests will use the 300 kW dynamometer the university has since installed, which controls load and measures torque accurately.

Testing

The no-load test was the first time the battery pack, the Orion BMS, and the DTI HV550 inverter ran together. The goal was to confirm they communicated and that the BMS behaved correctly before the motor was loaded.

The loaded test ran more power through the system with the water brake resisting the motor. It showed one problem. The motor stopped at random after about 30 seconds, with no fault code from the inverter or the BMS. We worked through it.

I didn't document the results well, and I would do that differently. What the tests confirmed is that the tractive system worked and the team knew how to run and troubleshoot it. That was the goal before committing to an electric car.

What's Next

Future powertrain testing moves to the university's dynamometer, because the water brake's load limit is unknown. Driving the car also gives more useful data than the cart can. The custom hub motors in development will run on the 300 kW dyno before they go on a car.

CR29 Accumulator: Manufacture

March 2025 · Manufacture Validation · ~4 min read

Building Cyclone Racing's first battery pack from scratch: the aluminum housing, the five modules, and what went wrong.

Specifications

  • Configuration: 28s1p per module
  • Cells: COSMX CA95B0D0HD
  • 5 modules total

Context

CR29 was Cyclone Racing's first electric car. Its main high voltage battery pack, which the FSAE rules called the accumulator, had to be built from scratch, and nobody on the team had built one before. This post covers how the accumulator was made: the aluminum housing and the five battery modules that go inside it.

What an Accumulator Is

The accumulator is the car's main high voltage battery. The rules renamed it the "tractive battery" in 2026, but for CR29 it was the accumulator. It isn't one large battery. It is a housing that holds smaller units called modules, wired in series to reach the pack voltage. Each CR29 module holds 28 pouch cells in series with no cells in parallel, written 28s1p. Five modules make up the pack.

The Aluminum Housing

The housing is a welded aluminum box that holds the five modules, the BMS, and the high voltage wiring. The flat panels were cut from aluminum sheet on a waterjet, which cuts accurately and puts no heat into the part.

The design called for the side walls to be bent to shape instead of welded on as separate pieces. Bending would have been faster and made cleaner corners, but the bends weren't accurate enough and the walls didn't sit where they needed to. The bent sections were cut off and the walls were welded on as flat pieces, both the outer panels and the inner partitions.

Welding the partitions caused warping. The outer walls are 3.2 mm thick and welded fine. The inner partitions are 2.3 mm, the minimum the rules allow, and a continuous weld puts more heat into 2.3 mm aluminum than it can take without moving. After welding, the space for the modules was too tight in places and the modules wouldn't fit. The fix was to work the warped spots outward with a mallet until the modules fit. The housing worked, but it was out of spec.

Fitting each panel before tack welding took a lot of time, and the housing still didn't match CAD exactly. CR30's housing added tabs and slots on the panel edges so the panels locate themselves, which is covered in CR30 Battery Pack: Design and Manufacture.

Module Assembly

The 28 cells in each module come in four types, A through D, which differ in tab length and orientation. The type sets where a cell goes in the stack. A cell in the wrong position has a tab that doesn't reach its clamping plate or bends at the wrong angle.

The tab clamping plates had to be prepared before any cells were stacked. They are 3D printed plates that clamp the cell tabs and bolt them together electrically. The innermost bolts can't be reached once the cells are stacked, so the design has nuts pressed into the plastic. A press fit doesn't hold reliably in an FDM print, so each nut was bonded in with JB Weld and left to cure. The bonded nuts were meant to act like heat-set inserts and give the bolts something solid to thread into.

Assembly then went in this order. The first D cell goes into the stack, a tab block is laid over its tabs, and the tabs are folded up onto the block. The first A cell goes in and its tab is folded. A second tab block goes on with a ring terminal for a BMS sense wire, and a bolt goes through. That repeats with alternating A and B cells, and the last cell is a C. After the last cell the main terminals are attached and the stack goes into polycarbonate baskets, which support and insulate it.

The modules took about 5 hours a day for three days, or about 3 hours per module including cell stacking, tab folding, bolting, and wiring the BMS sense harness. That doesn't count 4 hours of sanding burrs off the tab blocks before assembly, or the time spent crimping fuses onto the BMS wires.

What Didn't Work

The JB Welded nuts were the largest problem. Some nuts weren't square in their pockets, so their bolts cross-threaded on the way in. A cross-threaded bolt breaks the nut free of the epoxy, which leaves a loose nut inside a module that is already stacked. Fixing one meant taking part of the stack apart. The bonded nuts also made the modules hard to take apart when they were reused for CR30.

Stack pressure was the second problem. Pouch cells need to be compressed while they cycle to limit swelling and dendrite growth. The module has compression plates, but the cell thickness in CAD came from the datasheet and not from measured cells. The cells we had were about 0.7 mm thinner each. Across 28 cells that is about 20 mm of lost compression.

The terminal bolts were a safety problem. The pack terminals could be reached from below even with their TPU plugs installed. Those are live conductors, which is a problem at inspection and for anyone whose hand ends up under a live pack.

Notes for CR30

The changes for CR30 that came out of this build:

  • Add self-jigging slots to the housing panels.
  • Redesign the nut pockets with a tighter interference fit so the nuts lock without JB Weld.
  • Measure real cells before setting the compression plate dimensions.
  • Fully enclose the terminal connections.
  • Reduce the bolt count.
  • Clean up the harness routing.

The accumulator worked well enough for testing.

2 Week Tube Chassis

March–April 2025 · Research Design Validation · ~2 min read

CR29's carbon chassis failed during cure. Over spring break, five of us designed a replacement tube frame in two weeks. It was never built.

How It Started

CR29 was designed around a carbon fiber monocoque chassis. To save time we built the mold from welded steel instead of the usual fiberglass. A steel mold is harder to seal, and during cure the vacuum bag lost pressure. Without vacuum the layers of carbon weren't pressed together and the resin didn't fill them. When the chassis came out of the mold the surface looked crispy and the layers frayed at the edges. It was soft and crunched anywhere I pressed on it. It couldn't be repaired.

That left us with a competition season, a complete drivetrain and suspension design, and no chassis. Over spring break five of us designed a steel tube frame to replace it.

The Sprint

There were no formal assignments. We wrote a list of everything the chassis needed and each person took tasks from it. We split the work by subsystem so nobody blocked anyone else.

The design started from CR25, the team's 25th car and one of the strongest tube frames it had built. The new frame, CR25-C, had to match CR29's critical dimensions. Those were the driver position, the overall envelope, and the mounting points for the motor, wings, and suspension. Those dimensions were already worked out for CR29, and moving them would have meant redoing finished work.

What I Worked On

I took cooling because I had designed CR29's cooling system. The tube frame changed where the parts fit but not how the system works. I also took the firewall, which I had worked on for CR29 along with the seat. FSAE rules are specific about what the firewall has to seal, and getting it wrong fails tech inspection.

Suspension was the larger job. A tube frame is heavier than a monocoque, so CR29's spring rates no longer worked. I chose new springs for the added mass, re-ran the blade simulations to check the geometry, laid out the hardpoints, and carried over the A-arms, anti-roll bar, and struts from the CR29 design. I changed as little as I could.

At the end of the design phase the team submitted the SES, the Structural Equivalency Spreadsheet. It shows that a chassis meets FSAE's structural requirements, and a car can't pass tech inspection without an approved one.

The Vote

With the design done and the SES submitted, the team had to decide whether to build CR25-C. The question was funding and time. The team leads voted and tied twice, and the meeting got heated. The final decision was to take CR28, the last IC car, to competition instead. Improving a car we already knew was a safer bet than building and debugging a new chassis in the time left.

CR28 went to Michigan and finished 5th.

Looking Back

The two weeks were worth it even though the chassis was never built. Five people worked from one list with a fixed deadline and no approvals to wait on, and everyone just worked. It is the closest I have been to a startup, and it's the kind of team I want to work on. I also learned that a finished design can still lose a vote. The frame was a good answer to a bad situation, and the team chose not to build it.
